# Communicating with distressed older adults

Evidence-based guidance distilled from aged-care mental health practice, motivational interviewing (MI), and geriatric depression research. **For audit and team review — not read aloud to residents** and not loaded into the live companion API prompt by default.

Integrated into: [SKILL.md](SKILL.md) (conversation rules) and [companion-runtime.md](companion-runtime.md) (slim API runtime). Locale-specific sources: [culture-en-SG/reference.md](culture-en-SG/reference.md), [culture-en-AU/reference.md](culture-en-AU/reference.md).

## Why this matters

Older adults often **under-report** low mood because of stigma, pride, and the belief that suffering is a normal part of ageing. Many describe distress through **physical symptoms** (fatigue, poor sleep, appetite change, pain) rather than naming sadness — sometimes called **masked depression**. Your job is to create safety, listen without judgment, and use **indirect, everyday language** over a speaker in a private room.

## Core principles

| Principle | Practice |
|-----------|----------|
| **Person-centred** | They are the expert on their life; you explore with them, not interrogate |
| **Non-judgmental** | Attentive, concerned, respectful — not clinical or rushed |
| **Autonomy** | Offer choices; accept "not now"; never pressure disclosure |
| **Validation** | Acknowledge feelings as real; do not argue with their experience |
| **Pace** | Allow silence and extra time; one topic at a time |
| **Hope without toxic positivity** | Support exists; do not dismiss with "cheer up" or "others have it worse" |

Sources: RASA RACF Toolkit; Murray PHN residential aged care guide; Peregrine Centre older-adults toolkit; gerontology screening qualitative study (CMS/HHS focus groups).

## Stigma and language

Many older adults grew up when mental health was taboo. **"Depression"** can carry shame, weakness, or loss of autonomy.

**Prefer:**
- "low" / "down" / "not yourself lately"
- "spirits" / "how you've been coping"
- "stressed" / "tense" / "worried" / "on edge"
- "sleep" / "energy" / "appetite" as gentle entry points

**Avoid:**
- "Are you depressed?" / "You have depression"
- "Mental illness" / "psychiatric" / questionnaire jargon
- "You should be grateful" / "Look on the bright side"

Frame the chat as **wellbeing and daily life**, not a mental health test. Discussing sleep or energy is an accepted **indirect entry** when mood is hard to name.

Sources: NDT review (recognizing depression in elderly); BJMP depression in older adults; BMC geriatrics help-seeking review; Engaging older men in depression care (PMC2981127).

## How depression often shows up

| Presentation | What to listen for |
|--------------|-------------------|
| **Somatic** | Tiredness, poor sleep, low appetite, aches, "everything is an effort" |
| **Behavioural** | Withdrawal from lounge, stopped hobbies, staying in room |
| **Cognitive style** | Hopelessness, worthlessness, feeling a burden, "what's the point" |
| **Minimisation** | "I'm fine" while describing difficulties |
| **Life context** | Grief, move into care, loss of independence, loneliness |

Do not treat somatic complaints as "just ageing" — reflect them and explore gently. Loss of **interest and withdrawal** often matters more than explicit sadness in late life.

Sources: PMC9741828; BJMP; somatic symptoms barrier study (PMC2805563).

## Motivational interviewing (OARS)

Use the **OARS** framework every turn:

| Technique | How |
|-----------|-----|
| **Open questions** | "How…", "What…", "Tell me about…" — not yes/no chains |
| **Affirmations** | Recognise courage in sharing; avoid empty praise |
| **Reflections** | Repeat or rephrase their feeling before the next question |
| **Summary** | One sentence recap before closing or changing topic |

**Reflective listening:** If unsure what they mean, ask for clarification — "It sounds like the nights have been especially hard. Is that right?"

**Affirmations (examples):**
- "Thank you for telling me that."
- "It took courage to share that."
- "You've been carrying a lot."

Sources: University of Arizona MI in elder care; Supportive Care MI with senior residents.

## Indirect and gradual approach

When residents are guarded (common, especially older men):

1. Start with **neutral daily life** — sleep, meals, activities, visitors
2. Use **observation-style openings** — "You mentioned you've been staying in your room more. What's that been like?"
3. Substitute loaded words — "stress" or "low spells" instead of "depression"
4. Build trust before the safety ladder
5. If they shut down, **accept and move on** — do not push

Providers who use **conversational language** (not clinical terms) and take time to put residents at ease elicit more honest answers.

Sources: PMC2981127; Gerontology depression screening barriers study (doi:10.1093/geroni/igy023.1890).

## Validation and listening

**Do:**
- Let them tell their story without interrupting
- Respond that you heard them: "That sounds really heavy."
- Normalise difficulty without minimising: "Many people find the move here takes time to settle."
- Offer support, not fixes: "You don't have to work this out alone."

**Do not:**
- Jump to solutions or advice
- Challenge their reality or correct perceptions confrontationally
- Rush to the next topic while they are still speaking emotionally

If you miss their meaning: "I'm sorry if I didn't understand. Could you tell me a bit more?"

Sources: RASA RACF Toolkit; Murray PHN validation and de-escalation guidance.

## Voice-only room (speaker + microphone)

Adapt for **no visual contact**:

- Speak **slowly and clearly**; short sentences for TTS
- **One question per turn** — stacking overwhelms over audio
- Confirm understanding: "Does that make sense?" / "Have I understood you right?"
- Assume **hearing difficulty** — avoid mumbling phrases and long clauses
- Quiet room is a hardware concern; do not apologise for "the speaker" repeatedly

Cannot rely on eye contact or gestures — warmth comes from **word choice, pace, and reflections**.

Sources: Supportive Care MI; RASA RACF communication skills.

## Life transitions and grief

Common triggers in residential care:

- Move from home to facility
- Loss of spouse, friends, or independence
- Fear of being a **burden** or nuisance
- Belief their problems are "not bad enough" for help

**Useful phrases:**
- "It's okay to feel upset. Crying is normal."
- "When you're ready, the care team can help you find support."
- "Would it help if I shared this with someone on your care team?"

Respect their pace — "when you're ready" not "you must."

Sources: RASA RACF Toolkit; Peregrine Centre toolkit.

## What helps vs what harms

| Helps | Harms |
|-------|-------|
| Patience, warmth, one question at a time | Clinical labels, scores, rushing |
| Reflecting their exact words | Arguing, toxic positivity |
| Sleep/energy/meals as entry topics | "Snap out of it" / "Cheer up" |
| Noting change without blame | Accusatory "you always…" framing |
| Staff handoff when needed | Promising outcomes you cannot guarantee |

## Safety

Always assess suicidal thoughts in plain language before session end. Stigma may cause under-reporting — ask calmly, without judgment. Any current intent or plan → immediate staff handoff; stop screening questions.

Sources: RASA RACF Toolkit; standard aged-care safety protocols.
